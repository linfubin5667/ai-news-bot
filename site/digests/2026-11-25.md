### RECAP: Training Long-Horizon LLM Agents via Reinforcement Learning with Context Compaction

A new arXiv paper introduces RECAP, a reinforcement learning framework that jointly trains an agent's policy alongside its context-compaction behavior — the summaries that replace interaction history when context budgets run out. The core insight is a behavior-distortion objective: a good summary should preserve the behavior the original history would have induced, and this objective decomposes into a distortion-based reward for summary generation plus self-distillation for subsequent actions. On Qwen3.5-4B, RECAP delivered absolute improvements of 4.6% on BrowseComp-plus and 4.3% on SWE-bench Verified over task-reward-only RL baselines. The work is practically important because long-horizon agents are increasingly bottlenecked by context limits, and treating compaction as a learned, reward-shaped skill rather than a heuristic is a meaningful architectural advance.

Source: [arXiv NLP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31258)

### ContextLure: Benign Conversation History as an Attack Surface for Indirect Prompt Injection

Researchers introduced ContextLure, a red-teaming framework in which an attacker silently observes an existing benign conversation, extracts evidence (task results, user preferences, operational constraints), and constructs an indirect prompt injection — without poisoning any prior turn. The framework selects evidence across User/System Preference dimensions with three policies and uses an Urgency/Priority matrix with four rewriting strategies to frame malicious requests as justified. It achieved 67.4% attack success on AgentDojo and 87.3% on InjecAgent. The finding is significant because it expands the threat model for agents from "malicious data in the pipeline" to "the agent's own legitimate history," complicating defenses that focus on input sanitization alone. Security teams deploying tool-using agents should treat conversation history as adversarial-readable context.

### Position Paper: Chain-of-Thought Is Not Explainability

A new position paper systematically argues that chain-of-thought rationales are frequently unfaithful to a model's actual hidden computations, and that this unfaithfulness is insufficiently taken seriously in high-stakes domains like medicine, law, and autonomous systems. Analyzing 1,000 recent CoT-centric papers, the authors find approximately 21.7% explicitly treat CoT as a load-bearing interpretability technique — building pipelines or safety arguments that would collapse if CoT is unfaithful. The paper makes three proposals: avoid treating CoT as sufficient interpretability without verification, adopt rigorous methods assessing faithfulness for downstream decisions, and report such assessments alongside interpretability claims. This is a pointed challenge to a widely used transparency practice and should influence how regulated industries document model behavior.

### SpatialCraft: Frozen Agents Self-Evolving Their Tool Orchestration for Spatial Reasoning

SpatialCraft presents a self-evolving framework letting a frozen (parameter-updated-free) tool-using agent improve its orchestration of spatial tools — 3D reconstruction, segmentation, geometric computation — from its own interaction history. It maintains two knowledge streams: Experiences (condition-action tactics distilled by contrasting rollouts of the same task) and Skills (multi-step procedures with explicit activation and termination conditions), coupled via a Contrastive Utility Score that randomly withholds experiences to isolate their contribution from task difficulty. Across five spatial benchmarks and four backbones, the framework achieved the best accuracy in all 20 settings. The result is notable as evidence that memory-and-orchestration learning can substitute for fine-tuning in tool-heavy multimodal agents, with direct implications for cost-efficient agent deployment.

Source: [arXiv AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31475)

### Irreducibility Theorem: Why Agent Governance Cannot Be a Single Layer

A new paper reframes autonomous agent governance from "enforcing correct execution" to the prior structural question of "which agents are allowed to act," formalizing allocation as a first-class governance constraint. Through impossibility results — including Sybil amplification and incompatibility of desirable allocation properties — the authors show trade-offs between fairness, efficiency, and strategy-proofness are unavoidable, and that these constraints propagate across execution atomicity, state enforcement, and behavioral drift. The central result is an irreducibility theorem: under finite observability, no governance architecture can collapse temporal, state, behavioral, and population dimensions without loss of correctness or stability. Empirical ablations confirm that removing or approximating any layer yields systematic failure modes. This provides rigorous scaffolding for a problem the industry has largely handled ad hoc.

Source: [arXiv AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31284)

### The Date You Didn't Set: System-Prompt Dates as a Hidden Variable in LLM Evaluation

This paper documents a subtle but widespread reproducibility hazard: many instruction-tuned models automatically inject today's date into the system prompt, meaning an unchanged evaluation sends a different prompt each day. Across six models and four benchmarks with all else fixed, two plausible dates (2024–2026) flipped a median 6.6% of test items between correct and incorrect; a grammar-constrained GCG search for adversarial dates cost Llama-3.x models up to 36.5 points of held-out accuracy. Date-switching also altered LLM-judge verdicts and undid 15–32% of reproduced jailbreak successes on Llama-3.2-3B. Strikingly, none of the eleven audited evaluation stacks pins the date. The authors recommend pinning, logging, and reporting the date — a cheap fix for a pervasive benchmark-integrity problem.

Source: [arXiv NLP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31254)

### FreDraft: Directly Maximizing Expected Accepted Length for Speculative Decoding

FreDraft proposes an on-policy training method for speculative decoding that directly maximizes the expected accepted prefix length of draft tokens, rather than the conventional token-level distribution alignment. The objective accounts for first-rejection truncation — once a draft token is rejected, all subsequent drafts are discarded — and induces prefix-aware credit assignment weighting each token by its contribution to acceptance of current and subsequent tokens. The method analytically marginalizes token-level acceptance rate to reduce gradient variance, and redistributes part of the distribution-level gradient into an explicit penalty for overestimated token probabilities. Experiments across multiple benchmarks and target models show improved accepted length and decoding speedup with faster on-policy convergence. Inference-efficiency gains of this kind compound across every deployment of a given model.

Source: [arXiv NLP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31256)
